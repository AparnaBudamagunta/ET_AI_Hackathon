Table 1 - Products - The master catalog of everything the retailer sells. Every other table connects back to this one.
It is the foundation of the entire system.

Every Column
sku_id
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Unique code for each product
Why:    Every table uses this to refer to a product
        Instead of repeating the full product name everywhere, we use a short code
        Products table is where you decode the code
Example: SKU_BEV_001 = Coca-Cola 2L India


product_name
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Human readable name of the product
Why:    What appears in the output report
        "Promote Coca-Cola 2L" not "Promote SKU_BEV_001"
Example: "Coca-Cola 2L"


category
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which product family this belongs to
Why:    Scope filter — when user says "build a promotion for beverages" system filters to category = beverages
        Also used for cannibalization
        (substitutes are usually in same category)
Example: beverages, stationery, electronics, apparel, fmcg


geography
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which country/market this product is sold in
Why:    Same product can exist in multiple markets
        Coca-Cola is sold in India AND USA
        But pricing, competitors, segments are different
        Geography ensures we never mix markets
Example: India, USA, Germany


currency
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   The currency for this product's market
Why:    Global system — cannot compare ₹89 with $2.99
        Every financial calculation uses the right currency for the right market
        Output report shows the right symbol
Example: INR for India, USD for USA, EUR for Germany


base_price
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   The normal selling price before any promotion
Why:    This is what we discount FROM
        Discount optimization step calculates how much below this price can we go while staying above margin constraint
Example: ₹89 for Coca-Cola 2L India


cogs (Cost of Goods Sold)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   What it costs us to buy or make one unit
Why:    The absolute floor — we can never sell below this price or we lose money on every unit
        Margin = (base_price - cogs) / base_price
        Constraint check: promo_price must be > cogs
Example: ₹45 for Coca-Cola 2L
         We buy it for ₹45, sell for ₹89
         Margin = (89-45)/89 = 49.4%


brand
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which company makes this product
Why:    Used in cannibalization analysis
        Same brand products more likely to cannibalize each other
        Coca-Cola and Minute Maid are both
        Coca-Cola brand → higher switching risk
        Coca-Cola and Tropicana are different
        brands → lower switching risk
Example: Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, HUL, P&G

Why These Columns and Not Others

DELIBERATELY LEFT OUT:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Product description    → Not needed for promotion decisions
Product image URL      → UI feature, not AI engine input
Supplier details       → Supply chain, not promotion planning
Weight / dimensions    → Logistics, not promotion planning
Barcode / EAN          → POS system detail, not needed here
Tax rate               → Can be added in v2 for margin calc
Shelf location         → Store ops, not promotion planning

RULE:
Only what the AI engine needs to make a promotion decision lives in this table. Nothing more.

How This Table Is Used
───────────────────────
STEP 1 — Product selection:
  Joined with inventory_status to get margin and price for scoring

STEP 2 — Mechanism design:
  base_price and cogs used to calculate what discount depth is financially viable

STEP 3 — Discount optimisation:
  cogs is the floor for all calculations
  base_price is the starting point

STEP 4 — Cannibalization:
  brand used to estimate switching risk category used to find substitute products

OUTPUT:
  product_name shown in all 11 sections currency shown in all financial figure's, geography used to filter regional plan

Table 2 - Inventory
BUSINESS QUESTION THIS TABLE ANSWERS:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
"Which products NEED to be promoted because we have too much stock sitting in the warehouse costing us money?"

Ans - Inventory is the first filter in our 9-step promotion planning process. Before the AI reasons about anything — we need to know what stock reality looks like.

Every Column
sku_id
──────────
WHAT IT IS:   Unique identifier for each product
BUSINESS USE: Links inventory to the product catalog
              (joins to products table to get name, price, category, geography)
EXAMPLE:      SKU_BEV_001 = Coca-Cola 2L India

current_stock
──────────
WHAT IT IS:   How many units are physically sitting in the warehouse right now
BUSINESS USE: Tells us the scale of the inventory problem or opportunity
EXAMPLE:      1840 units of Coca-Cola 2L

WHY IT MATTERS:
  Too high → money tied up in warehouse storage costs accumulating risk of expiry or obsolescence
             → PROMOTE to clear it

  Too low  → risk of stocking out during promo
             → DO NOT promote aggressively
             → promo would fail on day 1

target_days_cover
────────────────────
WHAT IT IS:   How many days of stock the business WANTS to hold at any given time
BUSINESS USE: The benchmark for what is healthy
EXAMPLE:      30 days — we want 30 day's worth of stock at all times

WHY 30 DAYS?
  Industry standard for most retail categories
  Enough buffer for supply chain delays
  Not so much that cash is tied up unnecessarily
  Retailer configures this in constraints.yaml
  Different categories can have different targets:
    Fresh produce:   7 days
    FMCG:           30 days
    Electronics:    45 days
    Apparel:        60 days

avg_daily_sales
────────────────────
WHAT IT IS:   How many units this product sells on an average day with no promotion
BUSINESS USE: The denominator in the most important calculation in inventory planning

EXAMPLE:      Coca-Cola 2L sells 20 units/day normally

WHY IT MATTERS:
  This is how we calculate days_cover:
  current_stock / avg_daily_sales = days_cover
  1840 / 20 = 92 days of stock

  Without this number:
  → We cannot tell if 1840 units is too much or too little
  → 1840 units for a product selling 200/day is only 9 days — dangerously low
  → 1840 units for a product selling 20/day is 92 days — dangerously high

  This is the context that makes stock meaningful.

days_cover (calculated)
────────────────────
WHAT IT IS:   current_stock / avg_daily_sales
BUSINESS USE: The single most important inventory metric
              "How many days will current stock last at normal sales rate?"

EXAMPLE:      1840 / 20 = 92 days cover

INTERPRETATION:
  < 15 days:  LOW STOCK    → do not promote risk of stocking out
  15-45 days: HEALTHY      → promote cautiously or don't promote
  > 45 days:  OVERSTOCKED → promote aggressively need to clear stock

stock_status (calculated)
────────────────────
WHAT IT IS:   OVERSTOCKED / HEALTHY / LOW_STOCK
BUSINESS USE: Simple flag for the AI engine

OVERSTOCKED:  days_cover > target × 1.5 (more than 45 days when target is 30)
              → Strong candidate for promotion

HEALTHY:      days_cover between 15 and 45 days
              → Promote only if other reasons exist (traffic driver, seasonal opportunity)

LOW_STOCK:    days_cover < 15 days
              → Hard block on promotion
              → Guardrail will reject this product

How This Table Is Used in Our AI Engine
────────────────────────────────────────
STEP 1 — Product Selection:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
AI looks at inventory_status table
Filters to OVERSTOCKED products first
These are the primary candidates

Joins with products table to get: price, margin, category, geography

Scores each product:
  High days_cover  → higher promotion score
  High margin      → higher promotion score
  Right category   → higher promotion score
  Right geography  → higher promotion score

STEP 6 — Geographic customisation:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Checks regional stock before recommending a promotion in a specific region

"Do we have enough stock in Indian warehouses to run this promotion in Mumbai and Delhi?"

If stock < expected promo volume → exclude region

STEP 9 — Simulation:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PySpark uses current_stock as the ceiling for maximum units that can be sold

Simulation cannot recommend selling more units than physically exist in stock

GUARDRAIL:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
If a product has LOW_STOCK status → hard block → AI cannot recommend it regardless of how good the margin is

Open Question - What if the stock is on the way to the inventory, can we give this option from the chat? Are we going to have scenario based changes?

Real Business Example
────────────────────
SCENARIO:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SKU_STA_001 — Classmate Notebook India
  current_stock:     3,200 units
  avg_daily_sales:   40 units/day
  days_cover:        80 days
  target:            30 days
  status:            OVERSTOCKED

BUSINESS REALITY:
  We have 80 days of stock
  We only want 30 days
  We have 50 days of EXCESS stock
  = 50 × 40 = 2,000 excess units
  
  At ₹45 price → ₹90,000 tied up in excess stock
  Storage cost accumulating daily
  Back to school season ending soon
  → Stock will become even harder to move

AI DECISION:
  High promotion score - Recommend aggressive clearance promotion
  Before back-to-school season ends - Bundle with Reynolds Pen Pack (complement)
  Target price-sensitive segment
  Run in India only (that is where the stock is)

Why These Columns and Not Others
────────────────────────────────────────
WHAT WE DELIBERATELY LEFT OUT:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Warehouse location    → v2 feature (regional warehouses)
                        For hackathon — assume one warehouse per geography

Expiry date           → relevant for perishables
                        our sample data is non-perishable
                        add in v2 for grocery retailers

Last reorder date     → supply chain feature
                        beyond hackathon scope

Shrinkage / damage    → operations feature
                        not needed for promotion planning

Seasonal adjustment   → handled by holiday calendar
                        not needed in inventory table
                        AI uses holidays config instead

RULE WE FOLLOWED:
  Only columns the AI engine actually uses in its 9-step reasoning process
  Nothing extra that adds complexity without adding decision-making value

Summary
────────────────────
INVENTORY TABLE PURPOSE:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Answers: "What needs to be promoted and what cannot be promoted?"

Four columns tell the complete story:
  current_stock      → scale of the situation
  avg_daily_sales    → context for that scale
  target_days_cover  → what healthy looks like
  days_cover         → how far from healthy we are

One derived column acts as the AI filter:
  stock_status       → OVERSTOCKED / HEALTHY / LOW_STOCK

This is the first gate every product must pass before the AI even considers it for promotion.

Overstocked - When we have more stock in the inventory than required its overstock.
Low Stock -  When we have less stock in the inventory than required its lowstock.
    What is required - This is something we provide from target_days_cover in inventory table
    We need to have amount of stock when compared with avg daily sales happening less than or equal to target_days_cover
        CASE
            WHEN CAST(current_stock / avg_daily_sales AS INTEGER)
                 > target_days_cover * 1.5
            THEN 'OVERSTOCKED'
            WHEN CAST(current_stock / avg_daily_sales AS INTEGER)
                 < target_days_cover * 0.5
            THEN 'LOW_STOCK'
            ELSE 'HEALTHY'
        END as stock_status

Table 3 - competitor_prices
What It Is - A snapshot of what competitors are currently charging for the same or similar products in each geography.

This is the market intelligence layer.
Without it the system plans promotions in a vacuum — not knowing if our price is already better or worse than the market.

Every Column
sku_id
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Links competitor price to our product
Why:    We need to know which of OUR products is being compared to which competitor price
        A competitor sells Coca-Cola too — we track their price against our SKU_BEV_001
Example: SKU_BEV_001 (our Coca-Cola 2L)


competitor_name
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which competitor is charging this price
Why:    Different competitors matter differently
        DMart is a KVI benchmark in India (customers check DMart prices mentally)
        Amazon matters for online shoppers
        BigBazaar matters for mall shoppers
        Knowing WHO is cheaper tells us which customer segment is at risk
Example: DMart, BigBazaar, Walmart, Amazon


competitor_price
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   What the competitor charges for this product
Why:    The core comparison:
        Our price vs their price = price gap
        Positive gap = we are more expensive
        → Promotion needed to close the gap
        Negative gap = we are already cheaper
        → Promotion can be smaller or different

        This directly drives discount depth:
        If DMart sells Coca-Cola at ₹85 and we sell at ₹89
        We only need to drop to ₹84 to win
        Not to ₹70 — that would be wasteful

Example: DMart charges ₹85 for Coca-Cola 2L
         We charge ₹89
         Price gap: ₹4 — small, manageable


currency
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Currency of the competitor price
Why:    Same as products table — global system needs currency context
        Cannot compare ₹85 with $2.99
Example: INR, USD, EUR


geography
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Where this competitor price applies
Why:    DMart only exists in India
        Walmart only exists in USA
        Aldi exists in Germany and USA
        Geography ensures we never compare
        Indian competitor prices with USA products
Example: India, USA, Germany

Why These Columns and Not Others
DELIBERATELY LEFT OUT:
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Date of price capture  → v2 feature (staleness tracking)
                          For hackathon we assume fresh data
Store location         → Too granular for our system
Online vs offline      → Can add in v2
Promotional price      → We capture regular shelf price
                          Competitor promotions tracked separately
Stock availability     → Competitor ops, not needed

How This Table Is Used
STEP 1 — Product selection:
  Price gap calculated for each candidate product
  Large gap = stronger promotion case
  (we are much more expensive than competitor)

STEP 2 — Mechanism design:
  If competitor is running a straight discount → we choose a different mechanism (bundle) to differentiate rather than match

STEP 3 — Discount optimisation:
  Minimum discount = enough to beat the cheapest competitor
  Not more than needed

STEP 8 — Competitor response:
  LLM reasons about whether DMart or Walmart will match our promotion based on their historical behaviour

OUTPUT SECTION 7 — Competitor context:
  Shows price comparison table
  Our price vs each competitor
  Advantage or disadvantage per region

SCENARIO:
  SKU_BEV_001 Coca-Cola 2L India
  Our price:    ₹89
  DMart:        ₹85  ← cheaper than us
  BigBazaar:    ₹92  ← more expensive than us

Real Business Example
WHAT THE AI DOES WITH THIS:
  "We are ₹4 more expensive than DMart
   which is the price benchmark for
   mid-income Indian shoppers.
   We need to at least match DMart (₹85)
   to be competitive.
   Discount needed: ₹89 → ₹84 (5.6% off)
   This keeps us ₹1 below DMart.
   Margin at ₹84 = (84-45)/84 = 46.4%
   Well above 20% minimum constraint.
   Small discount. Big competitive impact."

Table 4 - customer_segments
What It Is - A description of the different types of customers who shop with this retailer — grouped by their shopping behavior, not their personal identity.

This is NOT individual customer data.
This is aggregated behavioural profiles.
Critical distinction for privacy compliance.

Every Column
segment_name
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   The name of the customer group
Why:    Different groups need different promotions
        What works for premium customers
        destroys value if applied to price-sensitive
Example: premium, mid_income, price_sensitive, loyalty_member, lapsed


geography
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which market this segment exists in
Why:    Indian mid-income is very different from US mid-income
        Different basket sizes, price sensitivity, response to promotions
        Segments are market-specific not global
Example: India, USA, Germany


category
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which product category this segment applies to
Why:    A customer can be premium for electronics but price-sensitive for FMCG
        We set "all" for hackathon simplicity
        In v2 each segment has category-specific
        behaviour profiles
Example: all (for now), beverages, electronics


segment_size
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   How many customers are in this segment
Why:    Bigger segment = bigger potential impact of a promotion
        A 1% uplift in a segment of 31,000 is more valuable than 10% in 1,000
        Used in simulation to estimate total volume impact
Example: 31,000 price-sensitive customers in India


price_sensitivity
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   How much this segment cares about price
        Scale 0 to 1
        0 = does not care at all about price
        1 = price is the only thing that matters
Why:    Drives discount depth decision
        High sensitivity → deeper discount needed
        Low sensitivity → non-price promotion works
        (gift with purchase, loyalty points)
Example: premium = 0.2 (barely price sensitive)
         price_sensitive = 0.9 (extremely price sensitive)


avg_basket_value
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   How much this segment typically spends per shopping trip
Why:    Higher basket = higher value customer
        Worth investing more promotion budget to retain and grow
        Also used in threshold promotions:
        "Spend ₹1,000 get 20% off"
        Set threshold just above avg basket to nudge customers to spend more
Example: premium India: ₹2,500 per trip
         price_sensitive India: ₹340 per trip


discount_response
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Demand multiplier when a discount is offered
        How many times more do they buy when there is a promotion
Why:    This is the elasticity at segment level
        Feeds directly into simulation:
        expected_sales = base_sales × discount_response
        Higher response = promotion more effective
        Lower response = promotion less worth it
Example: price_sensitive = 3.2x
         (they buy 3.2x more during a promotion)
         premium = 1.2x
         (they barely change behaviour for discounts)


loyalty_score
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   How likely this segment is to keep coming back regardless of promotion
        Scale 0 to 1
Why:    High loyalty = do not need to discount heavily
        They will come back anyway
        Low loyalty = at risk of switching to competitor
        Need stronger promotion to retain
Example: loyalty_member = 0.92
         (92% chance they return next month)
         lapsed = 0.20
         (only 20% chance — need reactivation)


description
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Plain English summary of the segment
Why:    Goes into the AI prompt so the LLM understands who it is targeting
        "High income, brand conscious, low price sensitivity"
        LLM uses this to choose the right mechanism and message for this segment
Example: "Budget conscious, high discount response, primary channel: in-store"

How This Table Is Used
STEP 2 — Mechanism design:
  Premium segment → LLM chooses gift/loyalty never flat discount
  Price-sensitive → LLM chooses flat discount deepest that margin allows

STEP 3 — Discount optimisation:
  Different discount per segment:
  Price-sensitive gets deepest discount
  Premium gets no discount (loyalty points)
  Lapsed gets reactivation discount

STEP 6 — Geographic customisation:
  Segment profiles differ by geography
  India price-sensitive ≠ Germany price-sensitive

OUTPUT SECTION 6 — Segment playbook:
  One row per segment showing: what offer, what channel, what priority

Table 5 — holiday_calendar
A forward-looking calendar of events that cause demand to spike or drop for specific categories in specific markets.

This is the TIMING intelligence layer.
The right product promoted at the wrong time is wasted money.
The right product at the right event multiplies results.

Every Column
event_name
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Name of the holiday or seasonal event
Why:    Used in output report to explain timing
        "We recommend this promotion for Diwali"
        Also used by LLM to understand cultural context of the promotion
Example: Diwali, Black Friday, Oktoberfest


geography
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which market this event applies to
Why:    Diwali only relevant for India
        Black Friday only relevant for USA and Germany
        Oktoberfest only relevant for Germany
        Prevents system from recommending a Diwali promotion for a US retailer
Example: India, USA, Germany


start_date / end_date
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   When the event begins and ends
Why:    Promotion must be LIVE before the event
        A Diwali promotion launched on Nov 8 (main day) is too late
        Should launch Nov 1 to capture full pre-festival shopping window
        System uses these dates to:
        → Check if event is upcoming
        → Recommend promotion launch date
        → Set promotion duration
Example: Diwali: Nov 06 to Nov 10 2026
         Black Friday: Nov 27 to Nov 27 2026


category_relevance
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which product category benefits most from this event
Why:    Not every event lifts every category
        Diwali lifts ALL categories
        Oktoberfest lifts BEVERAGES specifically
        Back to School lifts STATIONERY specifically
        System uses this to match scope category with relevant events
Example: "all" → every category benefits
         "beverages" → only beverages benefit
         "stationery" → only stationery benefits


demand_multiplier
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   How many times normal sales volume this event generates
Why:    The most important number in simulation
        Normal weekly sales × demand_multiplier = expected sales during promotion window

        This is what makes festival promotions so powerful — you are promoting INTO a demand wave not creating demand from nothing

        Black Friday: 4.2x normal electronics sales
        → A product selling 100 units/week sells 420 units during Black Friday week without any promotion
          With promotion: even higher

Example: Black Friday electronics: 4.2x
         Diwali all categories: 2.8x
         Oktoberfest beverages: 2.6x
         Regular weekend: 1.0x (baseline)


description
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Plain English explanation of the event
Why:    Goes into LLM prompt as context
        LLM understands WHY this event matters and tailors the promotion reasoning accordingly
Example: "Festival of lights — peak retail 5-day window. Gift giving, home decoration, new purchases are cultural norm."

How This Table Is Used
STEP 0 — Scope parser:
  User says "for Diwali" → System looks up holiday_calendar → Finds start_date and end_date automatically (User does not need to specify dates)

STEP 1 — Product selection: demand_multiplier joined to candidate products
  Higher multiplier = better time to promote
  Timing score added to product selection score

STEP 3 — Discount optimisation:
  During high-multiplier events → smaller discount needed (demand already elevated — no need to over-invest in discounting)

STEP 7 — Geographic customisation:
  Different events in different geographies in the same time window
  October: Dussehra in India
           Oktoberfest ending in Germany
           Halloween in USA
  Each geography gets event-appropriate plan

OUTPUT SECTION 1 — Recommendation card:
  "Promote for Diwali Nov 6-10 2026
   Expected demand uplift: 2.8x normal"

Table 6 — benchmark_elasticity
The cold start intelligence layer.

When a new retail company uses the system for the first time they have zero historical promotion data.

Without benchmarks the simulation produces meaningless numbers.
With benchmarks the simulation produces directionally correct estimates that get replaced by real data over time.

This is the Phase 1 data from our
cold start strategy (Gap 5).

Every Column
category
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which product category this benchmark applies to
Why:    Elasticity varies dramatically by category
        Electronics buyers barely respond to discounts
        FMCG buyers are highly price sensitive
        One benchmark for all categories would be wrong
Example: beverages, stationery, electronics, apparel, fmcg


geography
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Which market this benchmark applies to
Why:    Indian consumers more price sensitive than German consumers for same category
        Market-specific benchmarks are more accurate than global averages
Example: India, USA, Germany, global


price_elasticity
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   How much demand increases for every 1% decrease in price Elasticity of 2.0 means:
        10% discount → 20% more units sold
Why:    The core input to P&L simulation
        Without this number we cannot estimate how many units a promoted product will sell
        This drives the entire financial forecast

Example: beverages India = 2.1
         10% discount → 21% more sales
         electronics India = 1.4
         10% discount → only 14% more sales
         (people buy electronics when they need it not because it is on sale)


cannibalization_rate
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   What fraction of promoted product's sales gain comes from other products in the same category losing sales
Why:    Without this we overestimate promotion value
        If we promote Coca-Cola and gain 100 units but Sprite loses 20 units (both our products)
        Net gain is only 80 units not 100
        Cannibalization rate = 0.20 means
        20% of gained sales come from own products
Example: stationery = 0.22
         electronics = 0.28 (highest — people switch between similar tech products easily)


seasonal_uplift_base
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Baseline seasonal demand increases for this category even without promotion
Why:    Separates seasonal demand from promotion-driven demand
        Stationery always spikes in June regardless of whether we promote
        This baseline prevents us from claiming credit for seasonal demand that would have happened anyway
Example: stationery = 2.1
         (stationery demand doubles in June even with no promotion)
         electronics = 1.2
         (barely seasonal — fairly flat year round)


discount_response
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   How strongly customers in this category respond to discounts
        high / medium / low
Why:    Quick signal for mechanism design
        High → discount promotions work well
        Medium → bundle or threshold work better
        Low → non-price promotions only
Example: beverages = high
         electronics = medium
         (people research electronics purchase regardless of discount depth)


source_note
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
What:   Where this benchmark data comes from
Why:    Transparency for the retailer
        They need to know if they should trust or override these numbers
        Also important for hackathon judges who ask about data sources
Example: "FMCG India retail benchmarks — directional estimates consistent with Nielsen/Kantar published ranges"

How This Table Is Used
PHASE 1 (new retailer, no history):
  All simulation inputs come from here
  Output shows ESTIMATE confidence badge
  Retailer told to validate after first promotions

PHASE 2 (after 1-10 promotions):
  Bayesian blend: 70% benchmark + 30% actual
  Confidence upgrades to MEDIUM

PHASE 3 (after 25+ promotions):
  Benchmarks fully replaced by actual data
  This table becomes backup only

STEP 3 — Discount optimisation:
  price_elasticity used to calculate how much volume uplift a given discount will generate

STEP 4 — Cannibalization:
  cannibalization_rate used as starting estimate when no historical data available

STEP 9 — Simulation:
  seasonal_uplift_base combined with holiday demand_multiplier to get total expected demand during promo window



products + inventory Together answer: WHAT to promote

competitor + holiday Together answer: WHEN and at WHAT PRICE

segments + benchmark Together answer: WHO gets WHICH offer and HOW MUCH uplift to expect
