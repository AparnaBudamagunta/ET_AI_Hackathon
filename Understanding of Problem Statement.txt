Retail – Autonomous Promotion Planner:
Firstly, it's a planner
It should be autonomous

Promotions - used to sell their products by retail companies
For doing a promotion we need a strategy before planning the promotion

In general, on a high level every successful promotion strategy involves
1. complex optimization
2. coordination
3. product and market understanding
4. subjective judgements
All these are needed while teams meet commercial objectives and operational constraints

What needs to be developed as part of Hackathon?
Agentic planner
1. It should be capable of extracting or inferring with high degree of confidence
2. consider all relevant input parameters
3. make decisions on key dimensions of promotion strategy --> This means first user should know the reason for promotion they are 
planning, which lens are they considering doing the promotion etc.
4. This planner should also adjust to dynamic requirements as it builds the strategy and satisfy all relevant constraints imposed
by the parent company --> This means, a strategy is being developed and meanwhile some constraints like weather etc., pop up then the 
strategy should be adjusted accordingly.

Inputs to the solution:
1. Inventory - What is the inventory we have, we plan to have, we need to have etc.,
2. Competitor prices - who are our competitors in the market, what are their prices etc.,
3. Holidays - Are we doing promotions during holiday season or non-holiday season, if holidays which, when, how etc.,
4. Margins - What are our margins for this promotion, profit or loss, are we selling for lower prices than COGS, are we putting more
money into the promotion etc.,
5. Customer segments - Which customers are we focusing on, which channels are we using etc.,

Once these inputs are considered the planner should decide (Outputs of the solution)
1. Which products to promote
2. Discount amount
3. Duration
4. Target audience

The outputs must satisfy constraints
1. Minimum margin
2. Inventory clearance
3. Marketing budget

Feature required in the solution
1. Select which products to promote - 
      Here we decide what to promote
      Promotion should happen on right products for right reason.

      In general, there are 4 reasons to promote the product
      a) Traffic drivers - They bring customers IN
            These are products people already want and know the price of. 
            Discounting them bring people through the door
            Example: Walmart promotes milk, eggs, or Coca-Cola at a low price. 
            You come in for the deal — and leave with 10 other things.

            Key trait: High purchase frequency, widely known, price-sensitive customers.

      b) Margin Builders — Make money while you're at it
            These are products promoted alongside with traffic drivers
            These also have high profit margins
            In a nutshell, we are hiding profits in the basket
            Example: A supermarket promotes cheap bread but places full-price butter and jam right next to it. You buy all three.

      c) Inventory Clearers — Get rid of dead stock
            These are in general those products that are overstocked, seasonal or near expiry.
            Promoting these is needed because it gives company the cash and also frees shelf space.
            Example: Zara discounts winter coats in February to clear them before spring stock arrives.

      d) New Product Launchers — Build awareness fast
            New products need trial
            In general, people will not be willing to buy something that is never known or un-used. Promotions help them to try or buy
            at low cost or buy on offer or something
            Example: Lay's offers a "Buy 1 Get 1 Free" on a new flavor to get people to try it without hesitation.

      While selecting the product, we ask certain questions and there are certain reasons behind asking those questions as well
      Question asked - What we are trying to check
      a) Does it drive footfall? - Will people come to the store FOR this product
      b) Does it have acceptable margin? - Can we afford to discount it?
      c) Is demand elastic? - Will the price drop actually increase sales volume?
      d) Is it strategically timed? - Is it the right season/moment for this product?

      In general, we can see as below
      High demand + Price sensitive → ✅ Promote (Traffic Driver)
      High margin + Bought alongside others → ✅ Promote (Margin Builder)
      Slow moving / overstocked → ✅ Promote (Clearance)
      New to market → ✅ Promote (Launch Push)
      Low demand + Low margin + No strategic value → ❌ Don't Promote

      Goal of selecting products to promote - Don't promote what's easiest — promote what's most strategic. 
      Every promoted product should either bring customers in, make you money, clear space, or build future demand.

2. Determine the promotion mechanism
      Here we decide how to promote
      Promotion mechanism is the actual tool/offer you use to change customer behavior

      In general, there are 6 promotions mechanisms
      a) Price Discounts - Pay less - Here we simply reduce the price.
          There are different types in price discounts
              i) Flat discount - ₹200 off on this jacket
              ii) Percentage off - 30% off on all shoes
              iii) Slashed MRP - Show old price struck through, new price bold

          Example: Flipkart's Big Billion Days — products shown with original vs. sale price creates urgency and perceived value.
          Best for: Traffic drivers, clearance, price-sensitive customers.

      b) Bundle Offers — "Buy More, Save More" - Combine multiple products together at a better price than buying individually
          There are different types in bundle offers
              i) Pure bundle - Shampoo + Conditioner packed together
              ii) Mix & match - Buy any 3 for ₹999
              iii) Cross-category bundle - Toothbrush + Toothpaste combo

          Example: McDonald's Happy Meal — burger + fries + drink bundled. You spend more than you planned, but feel you got a deal.
          Best for: Increasing basket size, launching new products alongside established ones.

      c) Buy X Get Y (BXGY) — "Reward the Purchase" - Customer buys something and gets a reward attached to it.
          There are different types in BXGY offers
              i) Buy 1 Get 1 - Free	Classic BOGOF
              ii) Buy 2 Get 1 - Free	Encourages stocking up
              iii) Buy X Get Y - free	Buy a pizza, get a drink free

          Example: Domino's "Buy 1 Get 1 Free" on Tuesdays — drives sales on a slow day, builds habit.
          Best for: High-margin products (you can afford to give one free), new product trials, clearing stock fast.

      d) Coupons & Vouchers — "Earn the Deal" - Customer gets a discount only if they have a coupon — adds exclusivity 
          and tracks effectiveness.
          There are different types in Coupons and Vouchers
              i) Paper coupon - Newspaper insert with ₹50 off
              ii) Digital coupon - App-based code "SAVE20"
              iii) Loyalty coupon - Your next purchase gets 15% off

          Example: Myntra's app-exclusive coupons — forces customers onto the app, builds digital habit, tracks behavior.
          Best for: Targeted promotions, loyal customers, tracking ROI precisely.

      e) Loyalty & Points Programs — "Keep Coming Back" - Customers earn points/rewards over time, encouraging repeat purchases. 
          There are different types in Loyalty & Points Programs
              i) Points per purchase - 1 point per ₹10 spent
              ii) Tiered rewards - Silver → Gold → Platinum benefits
              iii) Cashback - 5% back on every purchase

          Example: Starbucks Rewards — every coffee earns stars. Stars unlock free drinks. 
          You keep coming back just to "not waste" your stars. This is psychological lock-in.

          Best for: Long-term retention, increasing purchase frequency, building emotional connection.

      f) Limited Time Offers (LTO) — "Act Now or Miss Out" - Scarcity and urgency are powerful psychological triggers.
          There are different types in LTOs
              i) Flash sale - 50% off — next 2 hours only!
              ii) Daily deals - Deal of the Day
              iii) Seasonal sale - End of season, festive offers

          Example: Nykaa's "Flash Sale" with a countdown timer — the ticking clock makes customers decide fast, reducing overthinking.

          Best for: Clearing stock quickly, generating buzz, reactivating dormant customers.

          Decision Matrix in general for choosing the right mechanism

            Goal                        →    Best Mechanism
            ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
            Bring new customers in      →    Price Discount / LTO
            Increase basket size        →    Bundle / BXGY
            Retain existing customers   →    Loyalty Programs
            Launch a new product        →    BXGY / Coupon (trial)
            Clear old inventory fast    →    Flat Discount / Flash Sale
            Track promo effectiveness   →    Coupons / Digital Codes

          The best rule in deciding the mechanism is to match the mechanism to the customer barrier
            Price too high? → Discount
            Won't try it? → BXGY / Free trial
            Buys once, never returns? → Loyalty program
            Needs urgency? → LTO

3. Optimize the discount and customize other strategies
        Any amount of discount can be given to any customers but then it would hurt the profits of the company. Hence giving the minimum 
        discount needed to trigger the desired behavior, while customizing strategies for different customers is really important.

        a) Optimizing the Discount
              i) Price Elasticity Test - tells you how sensitive your customers are to price changes.
                Elasticity = % Change in Demand / % Change in Price
                Elasticity Type --- Meaning --- Discount Strategy
                High elasticity --> Small discount = big sales jump --> Even 10% works well
                Low elasticity --> Even big discounts --> don't move much	Don't over-discount — try other mechanisms

                Example: A ₹500 commodity shampoo is highly elastic — drop it to ₹450 and sales spike. 
                But a ₹5,000 premium face cream is inelastic — a 10% discount won't move loyal buyers much. 
                A gift-with-purchase works better there.

              ii) The Discount Ladder - Don't start at your maximum discount. Test incrementally.
                  Step 1 → 10% off         (Does it move?)
                  Step 2 → 15% off         (Does it move more?)
                  Step 3 → 20% off         (Sweet spot found?)
                  Step 4 → 25% off         (Margins hurting now — stop here)

                Example: Amazon tests different discount levels across user segments on the same product during Prime Day 
                to find the "minimum effective discount."

              iii) Protect Margin With Thresholds - Instead of discounting the product, set a spending threshold to unlock the deal.
                  ❌ "20% off everything"
                  ✅ "Spend ₹1,500 and get 20% off"

                This forces customers to add more to their basket before getting the reward — you give a discount but recover it 
                through higher volume.

                Example: Myntra's "Spend ₹2,000, get ₹300 off" — customers who had ₹1,700 in cart actively add more items just to 
                unlock the discount.

              iv) Perceived Value vs. Actual Discount - Sometimes how you show the discount matters more than the discount itself.
                  Framing (show off) matters                  
                  Same Discount --- Framing A --- Framing B
                  ₹200 off a ₹1,000 product --> "20% off"	or "Save ₹200"
                  ₹2,000 off a ₹20,000 product --> "Save ₹2,000" or "10% off"

                  Rule: For cheap products → show % off (looks bigger). 
                  For expensive products → show ₹ amount saved (feels more tangible).

        b) Customizing Strategies by Customer Segment - One promotion does NOT fit all. Great retailers customize based on who the customer is.
            The 4 Key Customer Segments
                    HIGH VALUE          LOW VALUE
                   ┌─────────────┬──────────────────┐
            LOYAL  │  VIPs 👑    │  Habitual 🔄    │
                   ├─────────────┼──────────────────┤
            AT     │  Win-back 🎯│  Let go / nudge  │
            RISK   │             │  💤              │
                   └─────────────┴──────────────────┘

              i) VIP / High Value Loyal Customers
                  Who: Buy frequently, spend a lot, love your brand.
                  Goal: Retain them, make them feel special, increase spend further.
                  
                  Strategies:                  
                    Early access to sales before general public
                    Exclusive members-only pricing
                    Surprise & delight gifts (no discount needed)
                    Personalized recommendations

                Example: Sephora's "Beauty Insider" top tier gets early access to holiday sales — 
                they feel privileged, not discounted. No deep discount needed.

              ii) Habitual / Low Value Loyal Customers
                  Who: Buy regularly but small basket sizes.
                  Goal: Increase basket size per visit.
                  
                  Strategies:                  
                    Bundle offers (nudge them to buy more)
                    "Complete the look" / cross-sell promotions
                    Spend threshold discounts ("just ₹300 more for free shipping")

                Example: A grocery store customer who only buys staples — 
                a "Buy rice + dal + oil together, save ₹80" bundle nudges them to consolidate their shopping with you.

              iii) High Value At-Risk Customers
                  Who: Used to spend a lot, but haven't purchased recently.
                  Goal: Win them back — they're worth the investment.
                  
                  Strategies:                  
                    Personalized "We miss you" offer with meaningful discount
                    Exclusive reactivation coupon
                    Show them what's new since they last visited

                Example: Nykaa sends "It's been a while! Here's ₹500 off your next order" to lapsed high-value customers — 
                the ₹500 investment to reactivate a ₹5,000 spender is very worth it.

              iv) Low Value At-Risk / Dormant
                  Who: Bought once or twice, low spend, now inactive.
                  Goal: Either re-engage cheaply OR don't over-invest.
                  
                  Strategies:                  
                    Low-cost nudge (app notification, small offer)
                    If no response after 2-3 attempts → stop spending on them
                    Use them to test new promotions (low risk)

                Example: A D2C brand sends a simple "Here's 10% off — last chance" email. 
                No response? They exit the active promotion list. Smart budget allocation.

        c) Customizing by Purchase Stage - Different customers are at different stages of their journey with you.
            AWARENESS → FIRST PURCHASE → REPEAT → LOYAL → ADVOCATE

            Stage --- Promotion Strategy
            Awareness --- Free sample, referral offer, social media promo
            First Purchase --- Welcome discount "First order 15% off"
            Repeat --- Loyalty points kick in, bundle offers
            Loyal --- Exclusive access, personalized deals
            Advocate --- Referral rewards ("Refer a friend, both get ₹200")

            Example: Zepto's journey — new user gets ₹150 off first order → earns cashback on repeat orders → 
            gets early access to flash sales as a loyal user. Each stage has its own hook.

        d) Time-Based Customization - When you promote is as important as how you promote.
              Timing --- Strategy
              Slow days (Mon-Tue) --- "Tuesday Special" — drive traffic on weak days
              Peak season --- Reduce discounts — demand is already high
              End of season --- Deep clearance discounts
              Customer birthday/anniversary --- Personalized surprise offer
              Post-purchase --- "Buy again" reminder at the right reorder moment

            Example: Domino's "Tuesday BOGO" and Swiggy's "Late Night" offers — 
            both solve the problem of low demand at specific times, not random discounting.

      OPTIMIZE DISCOUNT          CUSTOMIZE STRATEGY
      ──────────────────         ──────────────────
      Test elasticity            Segment your customers
      Use discount ladder        Match offer to segment goal
      Set spend thresholds       Personalize by journey stage
      Frame it smartly           Time it to behavior patterns
      Protect your margin        Don't over-invest in low-value segments

4. Understand cannibalization
      One of the most underestimated risks in promotion strategy.

      What is Cannibalization?
      Cannibalization happens when the sales boost of a promoted product comes at the expense of another product you already sell — 
      eating into your own revenue.

      Analogy
        Imagine you run a pizza shop selling:
        Regular Pizza at ₹300
        Premium Pizza at ₹500
        
        You promote Regular Pizza at ₹200. Sales of Regular Pizza double.
        But Premium Pizza sales drop by 60%.
        
        You didn't gain new customers — your own Premium Pizza customers just switched down.
        Your promotion ate itself.

      In general, there are 3 types of cannibalization
      a) Product Cannibalization - A promoted product steals sales from a similar product in your own range.

          Your Product A (Promoted) 📈  →  Your Product B (Similar) 📉

          Example: When Lay's promotes its Classic salted chips heavily, sales of its own Magic Masala variant drop — 
          same company, same shelf, customers just switch flavors.

      b) Stockpiling Cannibalization - Customers buy in bulk during a promotion — and then don't buy for a long time after.
        You borrowed future sales, not created new ones.

          Promotion Week → Sales Spike 📈
          Week After → Sales Crash 📉 (customers already stocked up)

          Example: HUL promotes Surf Excel detergent at "Buy 2 get 1 free." Loyal customers buy 6 months worth. 
          For the next 5 months — zero sales from those customers. The promotion cannibalized its own future demand.

      c) Margin Cannibalization - Customers who would have bought at full price now wait for promotions — 
          destroying your margin without adding new volume.

          Before Promotion Era:  Customer buys at ₹1,000  ✅
          After Regular Promos:  Same customer waits for ₹700 sale 😬

          Example: Furniture retailers like @Home or retailers running "End of Season Sales" every 3 months — 
          customers learn the pattern and refuse to buy at full price. The promotion trained them to wait.

      How to Measure Cannibalization?

                                  Lost Sales of Other Products
        Cannibalization Rate =   ─────────────────────────────  × 100
                                  Gained Sales of Promoted Product

        Example:
        
        Promoted Product A gains: 1,000 units
        Product B lost: 400 units (customers switched)
        Cannibalization Rate = 400/1000 × 100 = 40%
        
        This means 40% of your "new" sales weren't new at all — they came from your own pocket.

    The Real Metric - Don't just measure promoted product sales. Measure total category sales.
    ❌ Wrong Question:  "Did Product A sales go up?"
    ✅ Right Question:  "Did TOTAL sales go up?"

    Why Cannibalization Happens? — Root Causes
      1. Products too similar         →  Customers easily substitute
      2. Promotions too frequent      →  Customers learn to wait
      3. No customer segmentation     →  Loyal buyers get discount they didn't need
      4. Poor shelf placement         →  Promoted item placed next to its own substitute
      5. Deep discounts on premium    →  Premium buyers trade down permanently

    How to Minimize Cannibalization?
      1) Promote Complementary, Not Competing Products - Promote products that are bought together, not instead of each other.
          ✅ Promote shampoo → place conditioner next to it (complementary)
          ❌ Promote shampoo A → placed next to shampoo B (competing)

      2) Target New or Lapsed Customers Only - Use coupons and personalized offers so the discount only reaches people who 
          wouldn't have bought anyway.

          Example: Zomato sends a discount only to users who haven't ordered in 30 days — 
          existing active users pay full price. Zero cannibalization of existing revenue.

      3) Differentiate the Promoted Product Clearly - Make sure customers understand WHY the promoted product is different — 
          so they don't just trade down from your premium product.

          Example: McDonald's promotes its McSaver meals as a separate value range — 
          not a discounted version of its regular menu. Clear positioning prevents premium buyers from switching.

      4) Limit Promotion Frequency - If you promote too often, customers stop buying at full price entirely.

          Rule of Thumb:
          ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
          Promotions more than once a month on the same product = You're training customers to never pay full price again.

          Example: Apple almost never discounts — scarcity of promotions makes full-price buying the norm. 
          Contrast with a brand that's "always on sale" — no one trusts their original price anymore.

      5) Use Non-Price Promotions to Avoid Margin Cannibalization - Instead of cutting price, add value — 
        so you don't train customers to expect lower prices.

          ❌ "20% off our premium moisturizer"     (damages price perception)
          ✅ "Free travel kit with our moisturizer" (adds value, protects price)

          Example: Clinique offers a "free gift with purchase" during promotions — customers feel rewarded but the base price is 
          never touched. Full-price buying habit stays intact.

      The Cannibalization Risk Map
                    HIGH SIMILARITY TO OTHER PRODUCTS
                                        │
                        HIGH RISK 🔴    │    HIGH RISK 🔴
                        (Switch + trade │    (Direct substitute)
                         down likely)   │
           LOW ──────────────────────────────────────────── HIGH
           MARGIN                       │                  MARGIN
                        LOW RISK 🟢     │    MEDIUM RISK 🟡
                        (Not worth      │    (Profitable even
                         promoting)     │     if some switching)
                                        │
                              LOW SIMILARITY TO OTHER PRODUCTS

      Safest products to promote: High margin + Low similarity to other products you sell.

      Cannibalization Checklist Before Any Promotion:
      ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
      ☐ Will this steal sales from my own other products?
      ☐ Will loyal customers stockpile and go quiet after?
      ☐ Am I training customers to wait for discounts?
      ☐ Is the incremental gain worth the cannibalization loss?
      ☐ Can I target only non-buyers to avoid self-cannibalization?

    A promotion that grows the total pie is a success. A promotion that just moves pie from one of your plates to another — 
    is just expensive shuffling.

5. Understand product relationships
      What Are Product Relationships?      
      Product relationships describe how the sale of one product influences the sale of another — positively or negatively.

      Entire product catalog as a web, not a list. Every product is connected to others in some way. Smart promotions exploit these         connections.

      There are 4 core product-relationships
      a) Complementary Products — "Goes With" - Products that are naturally used together. When one sells, the other should too.
            
            Examples:            
            Toothbrush → Toothpaste
            Printer → Ink Cartridge
            Bread → Butter & Jam
            Running Shoes → Sports Socks
            Phone → Phone Case + Screen Guard

            Promotion Strategy: Bundle them, place them together, or offer a discount on B when A is bought.

            Example: Amazon's "Frequently Bought Together" section is entirely built on complementary relationships — "You bought a               DSLR camera, here's a memory card + camera bag at a combo price." Average order value jumps significantly.

      b) Substitute Products — "Instead Of" - Products that serve the same need — customer buys one OR the other, never both.

            Examples:
            Pepsi vs Coca-Cola
            Store-brand rice vs branded rice
            Two similar moisturizers by different brands
            Android phone vs iPhone

            Promotion Strategy:
            Be very careful promoting one — you'll cannibalize the other (as we saw in the last topic). Promote the higher margin                 substitute.

            Example: A pharmacy promoting its store-brand paracetamol over Calpol — same molecule, higher margin for the store.                   Customers switch, store wins.

      c) Halo Products — "Makes Everything Look Good" - A premium or well-known product that elevates the perception of products               around it.

            Premium Product A (Halo) ✨  →  Lifts perception of Product B, C, D

            Examples:
            Apple iPhones on display make the whole Apple accessories range feel premium
            A luxury olive oil at the entrance of a supermarket makes the whole store feel upscale
            A designer collaboration piece in a clothing store makes regular items feel more aspirational

            Promotion Strategy:
            Don't always discount the halo product — just feature it prominently. Let it do perception work, not discount work.

            Example: Starbucks prominently displays its Reserve/premium range — most people buy the regular latte, but the presence               of ₹800 Reserve coffee makes ₹350 seem reasonable by comparison. This is the anchoring effect.

      d) KVI — Known Value Items — "The Price People Remember" - Products whose prices customers know by heart. These define your             store's price image.

            Customer knows the "right price" of these products
            → If your price is high, whole store feels expensive
            → If your price is right, whole store feels good value

            Examples:
            Amul butter (everyone knows it's ~₹60)
            Cadbury Dairy Milk 5-star (₹10/₹20)
            Petrol prices
            Onions & tomatoes in a grocery store
            Coca-Cola 2L bottle

            Promotion Strategy:
            Always keep KVIs competitively priced — even at low or zero margin. They are your store's price reputation.

            Example: Big Bazaar prices onions and tomatoes aggressively — not because vegetables make profit, but because if your                 onion price is right, customers assume everything else in the store is good value too.

      Advanced Relationships - used by sophisticated retailers
      e) Gateway Products — "First Step In" - Products that introduce customers to a brand or category, leading to higher-value             purchases later.

            Low-cost entry product → Customer experience → Premium upsell

            Example:
            Gillette sells razors cheap → makes money on blades forever (the razor-blade model)

      f) Habitual Replenishment Products — "Always Running Out" - Products customers buy on a cycle — consumables that run out and             need replacing.

            First purchase → Repeat purchase → Automatic loyalty

            Examples: Coffee pods, printer ink, pet food, baby diapers, protein powder

            Promotion Strategy:
            Acquire the customer on the first purchase (even at a loss) — future replenishment purchases are highly profitable.

            Example: Amazon Subscribe & Save — 10% discount for auto-replenishment of household consumables. Customer saves a little,             Amazon locks in guaranteed recurring revenue.

      g) Premium — Value Product Ladders — "Trade Up or Down" - Products arranged in a good-better-best structure within a category.

            Value (Entry) → Standard (Mid) → Premium (Top)
                 ₹199           ₹399            ₹799

            Promotion Strategy: Use the entry product to attract, then trade customers up to higher margin products.

            Example: Hotels — Basic room gets you in the door (promoted price), upgrade to sea-view/suite is where the margin lives.              The entry price is bait, the upgrade is the business.

      How to Use Product Relationships in Promotions?
      Complementary → Bundle & Cross-Sell
      Substitute → Promote the Higher Margin One
      Halo → Feature, Don't Discount
      KVI → Price Competitively, Always

      The Basket Architecture — Putting It All Together
      Smart retailers design the basket using all four relationships at once:
      A complete basket strategy:
      ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
      1. KVI priced right         →  Customer trusts the store
      2. Halo product visible     →  Store feels premium
      3. Traffic driver promoted  →  Customer walks in
      4. Complementary bundled    →  Basket size grows
      5. Substitute managed       →  High-margin version wins

6. Consider inventory constraints
      This is where operations meets strategy. The best promotion in the world fails if you run out of stock on day one — or if             you're stuck with mountains of unsold inventory after it ends.

      Why Inventory Constraints Matter in Promotions?
      A promotion without inventory planning is just a promise you might not keep.

      Two equally bad outcomes:
      Too little stock  →  Stockout → Angry customers, lost sales, damaged trust
      Too much stock    →  Dead inventory → Tied up cash, markdowns, waste

      The 4 Core Inventory Scenarios
      a) Healthy Stock, Slow Movement

            Situation: You have plenty of inventory but it's not selling fast enough.
            Inventory Goal: Accelerate sell-through before it becomes a problem.

            Promotion Strategy:
            Moderate discount to stimulate demand
            Bundle with a faster-moving product
            Featured placement + promotion combo

            Example: A clothing retailer sitting on 2,000 units of a mid-season jacket — not yet end-of-season, but moving slowly. A              "15% off this week only" with featured shelf placement clears 600 units without destroying margin.

      b) Low Stock, High Demand
            Situation: Product is flying off shelves. You're at risk of running out.
            Inventory Goal: Protect remaining stock, avoid stockout frustration.

            Promotion Strategy:
            Do NOT promote — demand already exists
            If promoting for brand reasons, cap the offer (first 100 customers, online only)
            Use this moment to upsell to a higher-margin alternative

            Example: During COVID, sanitizers and masks were in shortage. Retailers who kept discounting them ran out in hours,                   frustrated thousands of customers, and lost trust. Smart ones rationed and held price.
            
            Rule: Never promote what you can't fulfill.
            Stockout during a promotion = worst customer experience possible.

      c) Dead Stock / Overstock

            Situation: Inventory has been sitting too long. Tying up cash, taking up space.
            Inventory Goal: Liquidate fast — recover cash even at low margin.
            
            Promotion Strategy:
            Deep discount clearance
            Flash sale with urgency ("while stocks last")
            Bundle with a new arrival to give it context
            Last resort: donate, liquidate to a third party, or write off
            
            Example: Zara's end-of-season sale — they clear ALL old inventory before new collection arrives. They'd rather sell at                50% off than carry it to next season. Fresh inventory = fresh brand image

      d) Constrained Supply (Vendor/Production Limit)

            Situation: You want to promote but can't get more stock even if demand spikes.
            Inventory Goal: Match promotion intensity to available supply.
            
            Promotion Strategy:
            Limited quantity offers ("Only 500 units at this price")
            Pre-order promotions (capture demand before stock arrives)
            Geo-limited promotions (promote only in regions where stock exists)
            
            Example: Apple promotes new iPhone launches with pre-orders — they create massive demand signals before a single unit                 ships, then allocate stock based on pre-order data. Zero waste, maximum hype.

      5 Smart Ways to Align Promotions With Inventory
            i) Set Stock Thresholds Before Launching - Define minimum and maximum stock levels that trigger or stop a promotion.
            
                  Stock > 80% of capacity   →  Promote aggressively
                  Stock 40–80%              →  Promote moderately
                  Stock 20–40%              →  Promote cautiously, cap quantity
                  Stock < 20%               →  Stop promotion immediately
      
                  Example: Flipkart's Big Billion Day sets internal "promo-off" triggers — once a SKU drops below a stock threshold,                    the deal automatically deactivates. No manual intervention, no overselling.

            ii) Forecast Demand Before Setting Discount Depth - Before choosing your discount, estimate how much extra demand it                   creates. This prevents the worst outcome: a viral promotion you can't fulfill.

                  Expected Uplift = Base Sales × Demand Multiplier
                  
                  Example:
                  - Normal weekly sales: 200 units
                  - 30% discount multiplier: 3x
                  - Expected promo sales: 600 units
                  - Stock available: 450 units
                  
                  → Either limit the promo duration OR cap quantity at 450

            iii) Use Time as an Inventory Lever - Shorten or extend promotion duration based on stock levels.
                  Lots of stock + slow sales  →  Extend promo duration
                  Low stock + fast sales      →  Shorten promo, create urgency
                  Stock almost gone           →  "While stocks last" messaging

                  Example: Amazon's Lightning Deals show a progress bar of stock remaining — as stock depletes, urgency increases                       naturally. The bar IS the inventory signal.

            iv) Geo-Target Based on Regional Stock - Don't promote nationally what you only have regionally.
            v) Use Promotions to Rebalance Inventory - Inventory imbalance across locations is a hidden cost. Promotions can fix it.
                  Example: Amazon's regional warehouse strategy — Prime delivery speed is partially a function of smartly promoting                     products already warehoused close to demand clusters.

      The Hidden Cost of Getting It Wrong
      STOCKOUT COST                    OVERSTOCK COST
      ──────────────────               ──────────────────
      Lost sales revenue               Storage & holding cost
      Customer frustration             Markdown to clear later
      Competitor gains loyalty         Cash tied up, can't invest
      Brand trust damaged              Waste (perishables)
      "Out of stock" = broken promise  Discounting trains customers

      Inventory-First Promotion Checklist
      Before launching ANY promotion, answer these:
      ──────────────────────────────────────────────
      ☐ How many units do we have available?
      ☐ How long will stock last at expected uplift?
      ☐ What is our reorder lead time if we need more?
      ☐ Do we have stock in the right locations?
      ☐ What's our stop-loss trigger (when to pull the promo)?
      ☐ What happens to remaining stock if promo underperforms?
      ☐ Have we communicated stock limits in the promo messaging?

7. Geographically customize promotions
      Why Geography Matters in Promotions?
      The same product, in the same store, sold to different people in different cities — needs a completely different promotion.

      Customers differ by location in terms of:
      
      Income levels and price sensitivity
      Cultural preferences and festivals
      Local competition intensity
      Climate and seasonal needs
      Urban vs rural shopping behavior
      
      5 Dimensions of Geographic Customization
      a) Urban vs Semi-Urban vs Rural - These are fundamentally different markets — not just in size, but in behavior.
            Example: HUL sells the same Lifebuoy soap differently across markets — premium bar soap with loyalty rewards in urban                 Mumbai, a value multipack with price discount in Tier 2 cities, and a ₹5 sachet with free sample trial in rural markets.              Same brand, three completely different promotion strategies.

      b) Local Festivals & Cultural Moments - Different geographies celebrate different festivals — and buying behavior spikes around       each one.
            Strategy: Build a geo-festival calendar and align promotions to it — not a single national sale calendar.

            Example: Big Bazaar runs "Pujo Special" promotions only in West Bengal stores during Durga Puja — discounts on ethnic                 wear, sweets, home decor. A store in Rajasthan runs completely different promotions for Teej during the same week. Same               company, same week, different worlds.

      c) Climate & Seasonality by Region - 
            Example: A footwear chain promoting monsoon sandals in Mumbai in June while simultaneously running summer sandal                      clearance in Chandigarh — same month, opposite promotions, both perfectly timed.

      d) Local Competition Intensity - Where competition is fierce, you need sharper promotions. Where you're the only player, you             don't need to discount at all.

            Example: Reliance Retail in areas where D-Mart also operates runs sharper price promotions on KVIs. In smaller towns                  where they're the only organized retailer, they focus on assortment and availability — not deep discounting.

      e) Warehouse & Supply Chain Geography - You can only promote what you can actually deliver. Supply chain geography shapes             promotion geography.
           Example: Zepto and Blinkit run geo-fenced promotions — a "flat ₹100 off" might appear only to users within 3km of a dark             store that has excess stock of a product. Hyper-local, hyper-relevant.

      The Geo-Promotion Playbook — Step by Step
      Step 1: SEGMENT your geography
              ─────────────────────────
              Tier 1 cities / Tier 2 / Tier 3 / Rural
              North / South / East / West
              High competition zones / Low competition zones
      
      Step 2: MAP local demand signals
              ─────────────────────────
              Which products sell most in each zone?
              What festivals are coming up?
              What's the local climate doing?
      
      Step 3: MATCH promotion to zone profile
              ─────────────────────────────────
              Right discount depth for income level
              Right channel for media consumption
              Right products for local culture/season
      
      Step 4: CUSTOMIZE the message
              ──────────────────────
              Language (Hindi / Tamil / Bengali)
              Local references and imagery
              Festival-relevant creative
      
      Step 5: MEASURE by zone
              ────────────────
              Don't average results nationally
              A failed promotion in one zone + success in another = missed learning
              Track uplift, margin, and basket size per geography

      The Geo-Customization Risk — Don't Over-Fragment
      A warning: too much geographic customization creates its own problems.
      Risks of over-fragmentation:
      ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
      Operational complexity   →  Hard to manage 50 different promotions
      Brand inconsistency      →  Customers notice price differences, feel cheated
      Supply confusion         →  Wrong stock sent to wrong region
      Margin leakage           →  Deep discounts in low-need zones waste money

      The balance:
      National level   →  Brand promotions, flagship offers, big sale events
      Regional level   →  Festival, climate, competition customization
      Local level      →  Hyper-local stock clearance, geo-fenced app offers

8. Competitor awareness
      Why Competitor Awareness Matters?
            Your promotion doesn't just influence your customers — it triggers your competitors, who then influence your customers            right back.
      
            You discount    →  Competitor notices
                            →  Competitor matches or beats your price
                            →  Your advantage evaporates
                            →  Both of you just gave away margin for nothing
      
            This is called a promotional war — and nobody wins except the customer.

      4 Competitor Scenarios You'll Face
      a) Direct Price Match - Competitor copies your promotion almost immediately.
            You offer 20% off  →  They offer 20% off
            You go 25% off     →  They go 25% off
            You go 30% off     →  Everyone loses margin

            Example: Flipkart launches Big Billion Days → Amazon immediately launches Great Indian Festival the same week. Both                   companies end up spending billions in discounts, funding each other's price war. The winner? The customer.

            Smart Response: Stop competing on price alone. Layer in value they can't easily copy — exclusive products, better                     service, loyalty rewards, faster delivery.

      b) Flanking Attack - Competitor doesn't match your promotion — instead promotes a different product to steal your customers             while you're distracted.

            Example: You heavily promote televisions → competitor ignores TVs and runs an aggressive promotion on home theatre                   systems — the natural complement. Your TV customer walks into your store, then next door to buy the sound system.

            Smart Response: Always promote with the whole basket in mind. If you're promoting TVs, lock in the accessories, warranty,             and installation too. Don't leave flanks open.

      c) Competitor Promotes First - Your competitor launches a promotion before you. You now have to react.
            React too fast    →  Looks desperate, starts a price war
            React too slow    →  Lose customers to their offer
            Don't react       →  Risk losing traffic; but might be the right call

            Decision Framework:
            Is the competitor promotion on a KVI?        →  Match immediately
            Is it on a niche / low-traffic product?     →  Ignore it
            Are your shared customers highly price-sensitive? →  React quickly
            Is your brand premium / differentiated?     →  Don't react; hold position

            Example: When a budget airline drops prices, premium airlines don't panic-match. They know their customer isn't choosing on price alone. But               budget airline vs budget airline — every price move triggers an instant response.

      d) Competitor Exits a Promotion - A competitor pulls back — ends a discount, discontinues a deal. This is actually an opportunity.
            Example: When a local competitor ends its "free delivery" promotion, that's the perfect moment to launch yours — you capture their price-                  sensitive customers who were only loyal to the deal, not the brand.

      The Competitor Intelligence Framework
      To respond smartly, you first need to know what competitors are doing — systematically, not accidentally.

      3 Strategic Responses to Competitor Promotions
            a) Match (Price Parity) - Copy their promotion to neutralize their advantage.
                  
                  When to match:            
                  On KVIs where customers will switch for even small price differences
                  When you have the margin to afford it
                  When the competitor's offer is time-limited and you can outlast them

                  When NOT to match:
                  When it starts a race to the bottom with no winner
                  When your brand is positioned as premium — matching cheapens you
                  When the competitor is loss-leading and you'd bleed matching them

                  Example: Supermarkets constantly match each other on staples like cooking oil, sugar, flour — these are KVIs and customers notice. But a                   premium organic food store doesn't match a budget supermarket's oil price — different brand, different customer.

            b) Flank (Compete Differently) - Don't match their promotion — instead promote something adjacent that steals their thunder.
                  They promote:   Price discount on Product A
                  You respond:    Superior value proposition on Product B
                                  (which customers also need, and you do better)

                  Example: When Uber runs a fare discount, Ola doesn't always match it. Instead they promote their subscription plan — a different value                     proposition that locks in customers for a month, making Uber's one-time discount irrelevant.

            c) Hold (Strategic Ignore) - Sometimes the best response is no response. Stay the course.
                  
                  When to hold:                  
                  Your brand equity is strong enough that customers won't switch for a discount
                  Competitor's promotion is on a product you don't meaningfully compete on
                  Responding would cost more margin than the customers you'd lose
                  Their promotion is clearly unsustainable — wait for it to end

                  Example: Apple never responds to Android price cuts. Their customer isn't cross-shopping on price. Responding would signal insecurity                      and erode brand premium. Silence is the power move.

      Competitor-Aware Promotion Design - Beyond reacting — you can design promotions that are inherently hard to copy.
            EASY TO COPY                    HARD TO COPY
            ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
            Price discount                  Exclusive product / bundle
            % off                           Loyalty reward (requires your card)
            Free shipping                   Faster delivery (infra advantage)
            BOGO on standard product        BOGO on exclusive private label
            Generic cashback                Personalized offer (data advantage)
            One-time deal                   Subscription lock-in

            Example: Amazon Prime's free delivery isn't just a promotion — it's a structural moat. Competitors can match "free delivery" as a one-time                 offer, but they can't replicate the ecosystem that makes Prime worth ₹1,499/year. The promotion IS the product.

      The Promotion Timing Game - Smart retailers time promotions to avoid or exploit competitor activity.
      AVOID:   Don't launch a big sale the same week as a competitor's
               flagship sale — you split the customer's attention and wallet
      
      EXPLOIT: Launch YOUR sale the week AFTER theirs ends —
               capture customers with unspent intent and fresh budgets
      
      PREEMPT: Launch just BEFORE a competitor's expected sale —
               lock in customers before they even see the competitor's offer

      Example: Myntra's End of Reason Sale is timed to preempt Amazon's fashion promotions — they capture the fashion-first customer before Amazon's             broader sale begins.

      The Prisoner's Dilemma of Retail Promotions - This is the deepest insight in competitor-aware promotion strategy:
                          COMPETITOR
                       Promotes    Holds
                  ┌───────────┬───────────┐
      YOU Promote │ Both lose  │ You win   │
                  │ margin     │ big       │
                  ├───────────┼───────────┤
          Hold    │ You lose   │ Both keep │
                  │ some share │ margin    │
                  └───────────┴───────────┘

      Compete on dimensions other than price — service, loyalty, range, experience. Price wars destroy industries. Value wars build them.

      Competitor Awareness Checklist
      Before launching any promotion:
      ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
      ☐ What are competitors currently promoting?
      ☐ Are they likely to match our promotion?
      ☐ Can we differentiate rather than just discount?
      ☐ What's our response playbook if they undercut us?
      ☐ Are we promoting at a time that avoids their peak?
      ☐ Is our promotion built on something hard to replicate?
      ☐ Is this starting a price war we don't want to be in?

      The best promotion strategy is one your competitor can see clearly — and still can't copy.
      Price is always visible and always copyable.
      Build promotions around loyalty, exclusivity, experience, and data — things that take years to build and can't be matched overnight.

9. Simulate the promotion before launching
      risk management step
      
      Why Simulate First?
      A promotion is a financial bet. Simulation tells you the odds before you place the chip.

      Without simulation:    Hope it works → Launch → Count the damage
      With simulation:       Model it → Stress test → Launch with confidence

      Real costs of skipping simulation:
      Discount too deep → Margin destroyed, volume didn't compensate
      Stock runs out day one → Customer fury, wasted ad spend
      Competitor matches immediately → Both lose, neither gains
      Wrong product promoted → Cannibalization, no incremental growth

      5 Layers of Promotion Simulation
      a) Financial Simulation - The foundation. Before anything else — does this promotion make financial sense?
            The promotion looks profitable till all hidden costs are taken in to consideration
            
            Calc of The Break-Even Volume Formula is important in the promotion simulation
            
                                                              Fixed Promotion Cost
               Break-even volume =                   ────────────────────────────────────
                                       (Promo margin per unit - Base margin per unit × lost sales ratio)
            
            
            Simpler version:
            How many EXTRA units do I need to sell to cover the discount I'm giving away?
            
                                    Discount per unit
               Break-even uplift = ──────────────────────────
                                  Margin per unit at promo price
            
            
            Example:
            Discount = ₹200/unit
            Promo margin = ₹200/unit
            Break-even uplift = 200/200 = 100% more units needed
            
            → You must at least DOUBLE sales just to break even.
            → Is a 20% discount likely to double volume? Only if demand is very elastic.

      Example: A supermarket discounting cooking oil by 20% needs to roughly double oil sales to break even on margin. Oil is a staple with high                   elasticity — plausible. A premium face cream discounted by 20% would need to double sales — very unlikely, since face cream buyers aren't that             price-sensitive. Same discount, opposite conclusions.

      b) Demand Simulation - How much will sales actually increase?
      
                                              % Change in Quantity Sold
            Price Elasticity of Demand =   ───────────────────────────
                                              % Change in Price
            
            
            Example:
            Last time we ran 15% off → sales went up 45%
            Elasticity = 45% / 15% = 3.0
            
            Now planning 20% off:
            Expected uplift = 20% × 3.0 = 60% more units
      
            Always simulate 3 scenarios - pessimistic, base case, optimistic
      
            Decision rule: If even the pessimistic scenario is acceptable — launch. If the base case barely breaks even — redesign the promotion. If only              the optimistic case works — too risky, don't launch.

      c) A/B Test Simulation (Pre-Launch Testing) - Before going national, test small. Real data beats modeled assumptions.

            FULL LAUNCH                A/B TEST FIRST
            ─────────────────          ──────────────────────────
            100% of stores             10% of stores get the promo
            100% of customers          10% of customers get the offer
            Full budget committed      Small test budget
            Risk: Everything           Risk: Almost nothing
            Learning: After damage      Learning: Before full rollout

            How to Structure a Retail A/B Test
                  Step 1: Split your market
                          ──────────────────────────────────────────
                          Test group:     5 stores / 10,000 customers → GET the promotion
                          Control group:  5 stores / 10,000 customers → NO promotion
                  
                  Step 2: Match the groups carefully
                          ──────────────────────────────────────────
                          Same city tier, same demographics,
                          similar historical sales — so comparison is fair
                  
                  Step 3: Run for enough time
                          ──────────────────────────────────────────
                          Minimum 1–2 weeks (captures weekday + weekend behavior)
                          Long enough to see stockpile effect fade
                  
                  Step 4: Measure the right things
                          ──────────────────────────────────────────
                          ✅ Incremental sales (not just promo product sales)
                          ✅ Basket size change
                          ✅ Margin impact
                          ✅ Cannibalization of nearby products
                          ✅ Post-promo sales dip (stockpiling effect)
                  
                  Step 5: Decision
                          ──────────────────────────────────────────
                          Results match base case? → Roll out nationally
                          Results match pessimistic? → Redesign
                          Results match optimistic? → Accelerate rollout

            Example: Zomato tests new discount structures in 2–3 cities before rolling out nationally. A "flat ₹75 off" tested in Pune before becoming a               national offer — they measured order volume, margin, and reorder rate before committing full budget.

      d) Cannibalization Simulation - Model what happens to your OTHER products when you promote this one. Always simulate at the category level, never             the product level alone.

            CANNIBALIZATION SIMULATION MODEL
            ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
            Promoted product:        Product A (20% off)
            Potential cannibals:     Product B (similar), Product C (substitute)
            
            Historical data:
            Last time A was promoted → B sales dropped 25%, C sales dropped 15%
            
            Simulation:
            A incremental gain:      +300 units × ₹200 margin = +₹60,000
            B cannibalization loss:  -75 units × ₹350 margin  = -₹26,250
            C cannibalization loss:  -45 units × ₹280 margin  = -₹12,600
            
            NET margin impact:       +₹60,000 - ₹26,250 - ₹12,600 = +₹21,150
            
            Is ₹21,150 worth the effort + promo cost?  → Decision point

      e) Stress Testing - Push the simulation to its limits. What breaks it?

            Stress test questions:
            ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
            What if competitor matches within 24 hours?
            What if demand is 4x and we stock out by Day 2?
            What if demand is 0.8x and we're left with excess stock?
            What if the promotion goes viral on social media — can ops handle 10x?
            What if our supplier can't replenish in time?
            What if weather disrupts delivery during the promo?

            Example: During Big Billion Day simulations, Flipkart stress tests server capacity, warehouse throughput, and delivery partner bandwidth —                   before a single rupee of promotion goes live. The 2014 Big Billion Day wasn't stress tested well enough — the site crashed, stock ran out,                it became a PR disaster. They've never made that mistake again.

            Pre-Launch Simulation Checklist
            Financial simulation complete?          ☐
            Break-even volume calculated?           ☐
            Three scenarios modeled?                ☐
            Cannibalization impact estimated?       ☐
            A/B test planned or completed?          ☐
            Stock availability confirmed?           ☐
            Competitor response scenario modeled?   ☐
            Stress test done (ops, logistics)?      ☐
            Stop-loss trigger defined?              ☐
            Success metrics agreed upfront?         ☐

            Simulate first, spend later.
